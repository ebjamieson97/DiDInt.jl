Under the Hood

Most pertinent details of DiDInt.jl can be found in the DID-INT paper, however, some details that might be of particular interest to users of the DiDInt.jl package are elaborated upon here.

Four Step Estimation Procedure

The DID-INT estimator is computed in four steps:

  1. The means of the outcome of interest (conditional on covariates) are computed for each period for each state (denoted as $\lambda$ in the DID-INT paper).
  2. Within state differences between these $\lambda$ values (long differences) are computed for treated states and for their controls.
  3. An ATT is computed for each treated state $s$ in each period $t$ from its treatment time on, denoted $\mathrm{ATT}(s,t)$: the long difference of the treated state minus the (weighted) mean of the long differences of its controls.
  4. The (weighted) means of the $\mathrm{ATT}(s,t)$ values within groups give the subaggregate ATTs (more details on the choice of groups in the Aggregation section), and the (weighted) mean of the subaggregate ATTs gives the aggregate ATT.

More explicitly:

Step 1. In principle, the following regression is estimated to obtain conditional outcome means $\hat{\lambda}_{s,t}$:

\[Y_{i,s,t} = \sum_s \sum_t \lambda_{s,t} I(s,t) + f\!\left(X^k_{i,s,t}\right) + \epsilon_{i,s,t}\]

where $I(s,t)$ is a dummy variable equal to 1 if observation $i$ belongs to state $s$ in period $t$, and $f\!\left(X^k_{i,s,t}\right)$ is a function of covariates whose form depends on the common causal covariates (CCC) violation that is being accounted for (see Common Causal Covariates and Model Specification). The estimated $\hat{\lambda}_{s,t}$ values are the covariate-adjusted means of the outcome for state $s$ in period $t$.

Implementation

In practice, this regression is not estimated directly on the full dataset as this would be computationally prohibitive for datasets with many states, periods, and covariates. Rather, the Frisch-Waugh-Lovell theorem is applied in the case of homogeneous DID-INT, state-varying DID-INT, time-varying DID-INT, and two one-way DID-INT to recover covariate coefficients which can then be used in conjunction with the outcome and covariate means per $(s,t)$ group to retrieve the $\hat{\lambda}_{s,t}$ values. For two-way intersection DID-INT, the regression is run separately for each $(s,t)$ group. The resulting $\hat{\lambda}_{s,t}$ values are numerically equivalent to those from the combined regression shown above. More details on estimating $\hat{\lambda}_{s,t}$ can be found in the Estimating Lambda section.

Step 2. For each treated state $s$ belonging to treatment cohort $g$ (the states first treated in period $g$), long differences are computed between each period $t \geq g$ and the period immediately prior to treatment, $r = g - 1$:

\[\widehat{\Delta}_{s,t} = \left(\hat{\lambda}_{s,t} - \hat{\lambda}_{s,r}\right) + \left(\hat{\gamma}_{s,t} - \hat{\gamma}_{s,r}\right)'\,\overline{X}_{s,t}\]

The analogous difference is computed for each control state $c$ of the treated state $s$:

\[\widehat{\Delta}_{c \to s,t} = \left(\hat{\lambda}_{c,t} - \hat{\lambda}_{c,r}\right) + \left(\hat{\gamma}_{c,t} - \hat{\gamma}_{c,r}\right)'\,\overline{X}_{s,t}\]

Here $\hat{\gamma}_{s,t}$ are the covariate coefficients that apply to state $s$ in period $t$ under the chosen CCC violation, and $\overline{X}_{s,t}$ are the means of the covariates of the treated state $s$ in period $t$. These are the integrated long differences of the DID-INT paper (equations 46 and 47): the change in the covariate-adjusted outcome is evaluated over the covariate distribution of the treated state, which, because $f$ is linear in the covariates, only requires that state's covariate means. More on when the covariate term matters in the Integrated Long Differences section.

Step 3. For each treated state $s$ and each period $t$ from its treatment time on, the ATT is the long difference of $s$ minus the weighted mean of the long differences of its controls (equation 48 of the DID-INT paper):

\[\widehat{\mathrm{ATT}}(s,t) = \widehat{\Delta}_{s,t} - \frac{\sum_{c \in C(s,t)} v_{c}\, \widehat{\Delta}_{c \to s,t}}{\sum_{c \in C(s,t)} v_{c}}\]

where $C(s,t)$ is the set of controls: the never-treated states and, optionally, the states not yet treated in period $t$ (see Not Yet Treated Cells). In this step, weights $v_c$ are used (by default) according to the number of observations associated with each control long difference (see Weighting).

Implementation

\[\widehat{\mathrm{ATT}}(s,t)\]

is computed as the coefficient $\beta$ in the weighted regression of the long differences of state $s$ and of its controls on an intercept and a treatment indicator, $\widehat{\Delta} = \alpha + \beta\, d + \varepsilon$, where $d = 1$ for the treated state and 0 for the controls. This coefficient equals the expression above and the regression also yields its standard error.

Step 4. Each subaggregate ATT is a weighted mean of the $\widehat{\mathrm{ATT}}(s,t)$ values that belong to its group $j$, and the aggregate ATT is a weighted mean of the subaggregate ATT estimates:

\[\widehat{\mathrm{ATT}}_{j} = \frac{\sum_{(s,t) \in j} u_{s,t}\, \widehat{\mathrm{ATT}}(s,t)}{\sum_{(s,t) \in j} u_{s,t}}, \qquad \widehat{\mathrm{ATT}} = \sum^{J}_{j = 1} w_{j}\, \widehat{\mathrm{ATT}}_{j}\]

where $J$ denotes the total number of subaggregate ATTs and the weights, $w_j$, are scaled such that $\sum^{J}_{j = 1} w_{j}\, = 1$. By default the weights used here reflect the number of treated observations associated with each $\widehat{\mathrm{ATT}}(s,t)$ and with each subaggregate ATT.

No regression pools long differences from different treatment cohorts or periods: every estimate is an explicit weighted mean of $\widehat{\mathrm{ATT}}(s,t)$ values, each of which only compares states within the same period.

Common Adoption

When every treated state is treated at the same time (common adoption) the procedure is exactly the same, with a single treatment cohort. $\hat{\lambda}_{s,t}$ is estimated for every period, the long differences are taken relative to the period immediately before the common treatment time, and every aggregation option is available.

Integrated Long Differences

The covariate term in the long differences of Step 2 of the Four Step Estimation Procedure, $\left(\hat{\gamma}_{\cdot,t} - \hat{\gamma}_{\cdot,r}\right)'\,\overline{X}_{s,t}$, depends on the DID-INT variation:

  • Homogeneous and state-varying DID-INT: the covariate coefficients do not change over time, so the term is zero in every long difference.
  • Time-varying and two one-way DID-INT: the term is not zero, but it is the same for the treated state and for each of its controls (they share the time-specific coefficients, and both are evaluated at $\overline{X}_{s,t}$), so it cancels in $\widehat{\mathrm{ATT}}(s,t)$.
  • Two-way intersection DID-INT: the coefficients are specific to each state and period, and the term does not cancel.

Consequently the covariate term changes the estimates only when ccc = "int" is used with covariates. For every other ccc option, and whenever no covariates are specified, $\widehat{\mathrm{ATT}}(s,t)$ only depends on the $\hat{\lambda}_{s,t}$ values:

\[\widehat{\mathrm{ATT}}(s,t) = \left(\hat{\lambda}_{s,t} - \hat{\lambda}_{s,r}\right) - \frac{\sum_{c \in C(s,t)} v_{c} \left(\hat{\lambda}_{c,t} - \hat{\lambda}_{c,r}\right)}{\sum_{c \in C(s,t)} v_{c}}\]

Aggregation

In Step 4 of the Four Step Estimation Procedure, a choice needs to be made in terms of which $\widehat{\mathrm{ATT}}(s,t)$ values should be averaged together. For example, we could average the $\widehat{\mathrm{ATT}}(s,t)$ values of the states treated at treatment time $g$, which would yield the subaggregate ATT for $g$. Repeating this for each treatment time would give us the set of subaggregate ATTs based on "cohort" aggregation. Conversely, we could average the $\widehat{\mathrm{ATT}}(s,t)$ values of a particular treated state, $s^*$, in order to retrieve the subaggregate ATTs per treated state. The following table gives an overview of the aggregation groupings and the string value passed to the agg argument in the DiDInt.jl package. Every option is available for both staggered adoption and common adoption scenarios.

Whichever option is chosen, the aggregate ATT (agg_att) is always reported: it is the weighted mean of the subaggregate ATTs of that option. Because it is a mean of subaggregates, the aggregate ATT can differ across agg options. It is the same for every agg option when weighting is "att" or "both" and treated_weight = "treated" (the defaults), in which case it is the mean of the $\widehat{\mathrm{ATT}}(s,t)$ values weighted by their numbers of treated observations.

Aggregation by Periods Since Treatment

Each $\widehat{\mathrm{ATT}}(s,t)$ is already a comparison between a treated state and its controls within the same treatment cohort and period, so averaging them by periods since treatment does not require any adjustment for cohort-specific intercepts.

Cohort Aggregation and Group-Time Average Treatment Effects

The subaggregate ATTs of "simple" aggregation correspond to the group-time average treatment effects of Callaway and Sant'Anna (2021). Their group effects weight every post-treatment period of a cohort equally, whereas "cohort" aggregation weights the periods according to the weighting and treated_weight arguments. The two coincide when those weights do not vary over time, for example under weighting = "none".

With treated_weight = "treated", an $\widehat{\mathrm{ATT}}(s,t)$ is weighted by the treated state's observations in period $t$, so that a subaggregate ATT estimates the effect on the average treated observation of its group. With treated_weight = "population", it is weighted like a control long difference: by the observations of both the period $t$ and the period before treatment that were used in its calculation. The two options give the same subaggregate ATT when it averages a single $\widehat{\mathrm{ATT}}(s,t)$, and can otherwise differ, including for a single treated state observed over several periods (the ratio $n_{s,r}/n_{s,t}$ changes from period to period).

Policies

When treated states adopt different policies, DiDInt.jl can report separate ATTs for each policy. The policy of each treated state is given either by the pvar argument (the name of a column holding each state's policy, with missing values, 0, "0" or "" for never-treated states) or by the treated_policies argument (a vector with one policy per element of treated_states). If neither is specified, every treated state is taken to have the same policy.

Policies do not change which states serve as controls: the controls of a treated state are the never-treated states and, if notyet = true, the states not yet treated in that period, whatever their (future) policy. Policies therefore only matter for the policy aggregation options, which form the groups of the Aggregation section within each policy:

For these options the ATT of each policy, $\widehat{\mathrm{ATT}}_p$, is reported in the att_p column (on the first row of each policy) along with its standard error and p-values. It is the weighted mean of the subaggregate ATTs of that policy, in the same way that the aggregate ATT is the weighted mean of all subaggregate ATTs for the other aggregation options. There is no agg_att column for the policy aggregation options.

Setting policy_aggregation = true additionally reports a pooled ATT across policies in the att_pool column:

\[\widehat{\mathrm{ATT}}_{pool} = \sum_{p} \omega_p\, \widehat{\mathrm{ATT}}_p, \qquad \sum_p \omega_p = 1\]

with the weight of each policy, reported in the policy_weights column, chosen through the policy_weighting argument:

The heteroskedasticity-consistent standard error of the pooled ATT comes from the weighted regression of the $\widehat{\mathrm{ATT}}_p$ values on a constant, with degrees of freedom equal to the number of policies minus one (so two policies leave a single degree of freedom). Jackknife and randomization inference results are also reported for each $\widehat{\mathrm{ATT}}_p$ and for the pooled ATT; during the randomization inference procedure a state's policy and treatment time are reassigned together.

Not Yet Treated Cells

Optionally (not by default), long differences from treated states, prior to the state's treatment time, can be used as control long differences in the Step 3 computation of the $\widehat{\mathrm{ATT}}(s,t)$ values. For example, in a scenario with two treated states and 10 periods, State A being treated at period 2 and State B at period 8, then control long differences can be calculated for State B for periods 2-7 (before B's treatment) to match a subset of the long differences calculated for State A.

Using not-yet-treated long differences as controls is available by setting the notyet argument to true. A state that is treated in or before period $t$ is never used as a control in period $t$.

Ensuring Proper Comparisons

In order to best handle data with missing values or inconsistent time period coverage across states, DiDInt.jl only computes $\widehat{\mathrm{ATT}}(s,t)$ when the treated state $s$ is observed in both period $t$ and the period immediately before its treatment time, and when at least one control state is observed in both of those periods. Control states that are missing one of the two periods are left out of that $\widehat{\mathrm{ATT}}(s,t)$, and treated state-period pairs without any valid control are dropped. Because every subaggregate and aggregate ATT is a mean of $\widehat{\mathrm{ATT}}(s,t)$ values, each of which compares a treated state with controls in the same pair of periods, no estimate ever compares long differences taken over different periods.

Period Grid Construction & Date Matching Procedure

DiDInt.jl can handle data at various temporal frequencies including daily, weekly, monthly, and yearly observations. The package constructs a period grid and matches observations to discrete periods on that grid.

Automatic Period Detection

By default, if freq is not specified, DiDInt.jl automatically detects the appropriate period length from the data by examining the maximum distance between temporally adjacent time observations and identifies whether the data is best represented at a yearly, monthly, weekly, or daily frequency (or some combination thereof) and constructs period lengths accordingly. The automatic detection accounts for leap years.

Manual Period Specification

Users can manually specify the period length using the freq and freq_multiplier arguments. For example:

  • freq = "year" and freq_multiplier = 1 creates annual periods
  • freq = "month" and freq_multiplier = 3 creates quarterly periods
  • freq = "week" and freq_multiplier = 2 creates bi-weekly periods

The period grid is constructed starting from start_date and ending at end_date, creating evenly-spaced periods of the specified length. If the start_date and end_date arguments were not specified, then the earliest and latest dates in the data are used as the start_date and end_date, respectively. If start_date and end_date were specified, then any data falling outside the start_date to end_date range is dropped from the analysis.

Date Matching Procedure

Once the period grid is established, each observation in the data is matched to a specific period. The matching procedure follows these rules:

  1. Basic matching: An observation at time $t$ is matched to the latest period grid date that is less than or equal to $t$.

  2. Treatment boundary adjustment: If there exists a treatment time $T^*$ between two adjacent periods on the grid, observations are matched as follows:

    • If $t < T^*$: the observation is matched to the earlier of the two periods
    • If $t \geq T^*$: the observation is matched to the later of the two periods

    This adjustment ensures that observations are not incorrectly assigned to pre-treatment periods when they occur after treatment begins within that period.

  3. Treatment time matching: Treatment times themselves are matched to the first grid period date that is greater than or equal to the treatment time, ensuring treatments are associated with the correct period on the grid.

Treatment Times Outside the Data

A treated state is part of the analysis as a treated state only if its treatment time falls within the data used, that is, after the first date and no later than the last date (after any restriction through start_date and end_date). Otherwise, with a warning:

  • A state treated at or before the first date is treated throughout the data. It has no pre-treatment period, so no ATT can be computed for it, and it is not a valid control either. Its observations are dropped by didint().
  • A state treated after the last date is untreated throughout the data, and is used as a control like a never-treated state.

Each treated state can only have one treatment time: listing a state twice, or a gvar column that takes more than one value within a state, is an error.

Standard Errors and P-Values

DiDInt.jl reports three types of p-values and two sets of standard errors, for every subaggregate ATT and for the aggregate ATT (and, for the policy aggregation options, for the ATT of each policy and for the pooled ATT).

  • Standard errors using heteroskedasticity-consistent covariance estimators (HCCME): HC0, HC1 (default), HC2, HC3, or HC4 and p-values via a two-tailed test using a $t$-distribution with $n - k$ degrees of freedom, where $n$ is the number of observations in the relevant regression and $k$ is the number of estimated regression parameters. The desired HCCME can be selected via the hc argument.
  • Jackknife standard errors and p-values, described in more detail in the Jackknife section.
  • Randomization inference (RI) p-values, via a randomization procedure described in the Randomization Inference section.

There are some circumstances in which the jackknife standard errors and p-values or the randomization inference p-values cannot be calculated. These situations are noted in the Jackknife and Randomization Inference sections, respectively.

The standard errors using HCCMEs come from the following regressions:

  • $\widehat{\mathrm{ATT}}(s,t)$: the Step 3 regression of the long differences of the treated state and of its controls on an intercept and a treatment indicator ($n$ is one plus the number of controls and $k = 2$).
  • Subaggregate ATTs: the weighted regression of the $\widehat{\mathrm{ATT}}(s,t)$ values of the group on a constant, using the weights $u_{s,t}$ ($n$ is the number of $\widehat{\mathrm{ATT}}(s,t)$ values in the group and $k = 1$). A subaggregate ATT that consists of a single $\widehat{\mathrm{ATT}}(s,t)$ reports the standard error and p-value of that $\widehat{\mathrm{ATT}}(s,t)$.
  • Aggregate ATT: the weighted regression of the subaggregate ATTs on a constant, using the weights $w_j$ ($n$ is the number of subaggregate ATTs and $k = 1$). If there is a single subaggregate ATT, the aggregate ATT reports its standard error and p-value.
Caveats for the HCCME Standard Errors

The regressions on a constant treat the estimates being averaged as independent. $\widehat{\mathrm{ATT}}(s,t)$ values from the same period share control states, so these standard errors can understate the uncertainty of subaggregate and aggregate ATTs. The jackknife and the randomization inference procedure do not rely on that independence.

The single treated state in the Step 3 regression has a leverage of exactly one. HC2, HC3, and HC4 divide by one minus the leverage, so the HC2, HC3, and HC4 standard errors of an $\widehat{\mathrm{ATT}}(s,t)$ (reported under "sgt" aggregation, and for any subaggregate ATT that consists of a single $\widehat{\mathrm{ATT}}(s,t)$) are NaN. HC0 and HC1 are unaffected.

When an $\widehat{\mathrm{ATT}}(s,t)$ has only one control state, the Step 3 regression has two observations and two parameters: the model is saturated and the regression-based standard error is degenerate. In such cases, it is still possible to recover the heteroskedastic robust standard error using the variances and covariances of the $\hat{\lambda}_{s,t}$ terms. Given one treated state $s$ and one control state $c$, the variance of $\widehat{\mathrm{ATT}}(s,t)$ (without the covariate term of the Integrated Long Differences) can be decomposed into variance and covariance terms of the $\hat{\lambda}_{s,t}$ values:

\[\begin{aligned} \widehat{\mathrm{ATT}}(s,t) &= (\hat{\lambda}_{s,t} - \hat{\lambda}_{s,r}) - (\hat{\lambda}_{c,t} - \hat{\lambda}_{c,r}) \\[6pt] \mathrm{Var}(\widehat{\mathrm{ATT}}(s,t)) &= \mathrm{Var}(\hat{\lambda}_{s,t} - \hat{\lambda}_{s,r}) + \mathrm{Var}(\hat{\lambda}_{c,t} - \hat{\lambda}_{c,r}) \\ &\quad -2\,\mathrm{Cov}\!\left( \hat{\lambda}_{s,t} - \hat{\lambda}_{s,r}, \hat{\lambda}_{c,t} - \hat{\lambda}_{c,r} \right) \\[6pt] \mathrm{Var}(\widehat{\mathrm{ATT}}(s,t)) &= \mathrm{Var}(\hat{\lambda}_{s,r}) + \mathrm{Var}(\hat{\lambda}_{s,t}) + \mathrm{Var}(\hat{\lambda}_{c,r}) + \mathrm{Var}(\hat{\lambda}_{c,t}) \\ &\quad -2\,\mathrm{Cov}(\hat{\lambda}_{s,r}, \hat{\lambda}_{s,t}) -2\,\mathrm{Cov}(\hat{\lambda}_{c,r}, \hat{\lambda}_{c,t}) \\ &\quad -2\Big( \mathrm{Cov}(\hat{\lambda}_{s,t}, \hat{\lambda}_{c,t}) - \mathrm{Cov}(\hat{\lambda}_{s,t}, \hat{\lambda}_{c,r}) - \mathrm{Cov}(\hat{\lambda}_{s,r}, \hat{\lambda}_{c,t}) + \mathrm{Cov}(\hat{\lambda}_{s,r}, \hat{\lambda}_{c,r}) \Big) \end{aligned}\]

Further details on recovering the standard errors in such an edge case can be found in the section Computation of Edge Case Standard Errors.

Jackknife

There are two variations to the DiDInt.jl jackknife procedure. The default option is to calculate jackknife estimates of the ATTs while dropping one state at a time directly from Step 3 in the Four Step Estimation Procedure: the state is removed both as a treated state and as a control, and the $\widehat{\mathrm{ATT}}(s,t)$ values, the subaggregate ATTs, and the aggregate ATT are recomputed from the $\hat{\lambda}_{s,t}$ values of the remaining states. This is referred to as the "fast jackknife". The alternative method, the "true jackknife", calculates jackknife estimates of the ATTs while dropping one state at a time starting from Step 1. The true jackknife can be used in lieu of the fast jackknife by setting the truejack argument to true.

Note that for the ccc options of "state" and "int" that the true jackknife is always equal to the fast jackknife as the covariate effects are local to the state or state-time, respectively, so that removing a state from the dataset does not affect the estimated covariate effects at other states and thus does not affect the estimated $\hat{\lambda}_{s,t}$ values at other states.

The jackknife standard error is computed as:

\[\widehat{\mathrm{se}}_{\mathrm{jack}} = \sqrt{\frac{N-1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(\hat{\beta}_{(-i)} - \hat{\beta}\right)^2}\]

where $N$ is the number of states whose removal leaves the estimate defined, $\hat{\beta}_{(-i)}$ is the ATT estimate with state $i$ removed, and $\hat{\beta}$ is the original ATT estimate. This is the cluster jackknife variance estimator centered at the original estimate ($\mathrm{CV}_3$ in MacKinnon, Nielsen, and Webb (2023)). Jackknife p-values are computed via a two-tailed test using a $t$-distribution with $N - 1$ degrees of freedom.

A minimum of two treated states and two control states are required to compute jackknife standard errors and p-values; if this condition is not met, these quantities are returned as missing. The same minimum applies to each estimate separately: a subaggregate ATT (or the ATT of a policy) only has a jackknife standard error if the $\widehat{\mathrm{ATT}}(s,t)$ values it averages involve at least two treated states and at least two control states, and if it can be computed with any one state removed.

The states that are removed in turn are those that contribute at least one long difference to the estimates; a state that never does (for example one that is only observed before every treatment time) is not counted. If removing a state leaves a subaggregate ATT without any treated state (for example the only state of a treatment cohort), the aggregate ATT for that removal is the mean over the remaining subaggregate ATTs. Its jackknife standard error is still reported, with a warning, since it then compares aggregates over different sets of subaggregate ATTs.

Further, note that for the aggregation methods of "state" and "sgt", where each subaggregate ATT involves only one treated state, that the jackknife standard errors and jackknife p-values are never estimated at the subaggregate level: removing that one treated state leaves nothing to estimate. This limitation does not prevent the jackknife standard error and jackknife p-value from being calculated for the aggregate ATT under "state" or "sgt" aggregation, so long as there are at least two treated and two control states.

Randomization Inference

The randomization procedure used in DiDInt.jl follows the procedure described in MacKinnon and Webb (2020), although for the "state" and "sgt" aggregation options, there is an additional randomization step.

The DiDInt.jl implementation of the randomization inference procedure is as follows:

  1. The total number of unique alternative treatment assignments is calculated by $\frac{N!}{(N-n)! \prod_{m \in M} n_m!} - 1$, where $N$ is the total number of states, $n$ is the number of treated states, $M$ is the set of unique treatment times, and $n_m$ is the number of states treated at time $m$. (For the policy aggregation options, $M$ is the set of unique combinations of treatment time and policy.) The number of permutations considered during the procedure, $nperm^{*}$, is the lesser of this total and the user-supplied nperm value.
  2. If the total does not exceed nperm, every alternative assignment is used. Otherwise, $nperm^{*}$ distinct alternative assignments are drawn at random, each being equally likely. The assignments are drawn without repetition and never include the actual assignment; the seed argument makes the draws reproducible.
  3. Subaggregate and aggregate ATTs are then calculated for each alternative assignment, as if the states that received the treatment times in that assignment had been the treated states. The $\hat{\lambda}_{s,t}$ values do not depend on which states are treated, so Step 1 is not repeated. The distribution of estimates resulting from the alternative assignments is compared against the estimates computed when using the actual assignment of treatment times:

\[p_{RI} = \frac{1 + \sum_{r=1}^{nperm^{*}} \mathbf{1}\left(|ATT_{RI,r}| \geq |ATT_{Actual}|\right)}{nperm^{*} + 1}\]

This is the more conservative of the two p-values discussed in MacKinnon and Webb (2020). An RI p-value is reported for every subaggregate ATT, each compared against the estimates for the same group under the alternative assignments, and for the aggregate ATT.

Drawing Alternative Assignments

Every possible assignment is an arrangement of the treatment times (and "never treated") across the states. The arrangements are numbered in lexicographic order, distinct numbers are drawn using Floyd's sampling algorithm (Bentley and Floyd, 1987), and each number is converted back into its arrangement. This guarantees distinct assignments without having to draw and discard repeated ones. The actual assignment is skipped by drawing from one number fewer and shifting the draws at or above its number up by one.

Kernel-Smoothed P-Values

With few alternative assignments, the p-value above can only take a few distinct values. When the number of permutations used, $nperm^{*}$, is no larger than the ri_kernel_cutoff argument (99 by default), DiDInt.jl instead reports the kernel-smoothed p-value of Racine and MacKinnon (2007), which replaces the indicator function with a smooth function:

\[p_{RI} = 1 - \frac{1}{nperm^{*}} \sum_{r=1}^{nperm^{*}} \Phi\!\left(\frac{|ATT_{Actual}| - |ATT_{RI,r}|}{h}\right), \qquad h = 1.575\; \hat{\sigma}_{RI}\; (nperm^{*})^{-4/9}\]

where $\Phi$ is the standard normal cumulative distribution function and $\hat{\sigma}_{RI}$ is the sample standard deviation of the $ATT_{RI,r}$ values. This applies whether $nperm^{*}$ is small because few alternative assignments exist or because a small nperm was requested. Kernel smoothing can be turned off by setting ri_kernel_cutoff to nothing, false, or 0. If there is only one alternative assignment, or if the $ATT_{RI,r}$ values do not vary, the unsmoothed p-value is reported.

Ties

Although every alternative assignment differs from the actual assignment, an estimate under an alternative assignment can be exactly equal to the actual estimate. Assignments are unique as a whole, but a given subaggregate ATT only depends on part of the assignment: for example, the subaggregate ATT for treatment time $g$ and period $t$ only depends on which states are treated at $g$ and which states are controls in period $t$. An alternative assignment that reshuffles other states leaves that subaggregate ATT unchanged. Such ties are most common when notyet = true for early treatment cohorts with few states, as the set of not-yet-treated controls in an early period changes little across assignments. The aggregate ATT depends on the entire assignment and almost never ties.

Ties are counted in favour of the null hypothesis (they satisfy $|ATT_{RI,r}| \geq |ATT_{Actual}|$). Because two estimates that are equal in exact arithmetic can differ by rounding error, estimates within a small relative tolerance (about $1.5 \times 10^{-8}$) of the actual estimate are treated as ties.

This applies to the unsmoothed p-value. With Kernel-Smoothed P-Values, an estimate equal to the actual estimate contributes $\Phi(0) = 0.5$ to the sum, so a tie counts as half of an exceedance rather than a whole one.

Aggregation by State

When the randomization inference procedure is performed while the aggregation is set to "state" or "sgt", each subaggregate ATT involves a single treated state, and an alternative assignment does not determine which state should take its place when more than one state receives the same treatment time. There is therefore an additional level of randomization: for each actually treated state, one state is drawn at random from the set of states that the alternative assignment associates with the treatment time of the actually treated state, and is used in its place in every period. This is in order to preserve the structure of the "state" and "sgt" aggregation methods whereby only one treated state is considered at a time.

Computing ATTs During Randomization

For each alternative assignment, $\widehat{\mathrm{ATT}}(s,t)$ is computed directly as the long difference of the treated state minus the weighted mean of the long differences of its controls (the expression in Step 3 of the Four Step Estimation Procedure), which equals the regression coefficient without having to run a regression. The computations are carried out for all alternative assignments at once using matrix operations, which is why the procedure remains fast for large values of nperm. The same approach is used for the fast jackknife.

It should also be noted that the randomization inference procedure will not run unless it is possible to calculate the long differences for each $(g,t)$ used in the computation of the actual ATT values for every state, treated or not: under an alternative assignment any state can take the place of a treated state. If a state is not observed in one of the periods involved (which may occur in datasets with missing values or inconsistent time period coverage across states), the randomization inference p-values are returned as missing, with a warning. This safeguard is to ensure that the randomization procedure only runs if it is possible to make a proper comparison between the actual $ATT$ values and the $ATT_{RI}$ values.

Estimating Lambda

As mentioned previously, estimating the regression model shown in Step 1 would be computationally infeasible, especially for a dataset with many states, time periods, and covariates. For example, the number of columns in the design matrix for a two-way intersection DID-INT model would be $n_{s} \cdot n_{t} + n_{s} \cdot n_{t} \cdot n_{k}$ where $n_{s}$ is the number of states, $n_{t}$ is the number of time periods, and $n_{k}$ is the number of covariates. Consequently, DiDInt.jl takes three different approaches for estimating the $\hat{\lambda}_{s,t}$ values depending on the specific DID-INT variation. For each approach, the resulting $\hat{\lambda}_{s,t}$ values are numerically equivalent to those from the combined regression formulation shown in Step 1.

In each case the estimated covariate coefficients and the covariate means of each $(s,t)$ cell are retained, as they are needed for the Integrated Long Differences.

Two-Way Intersection DID-INT

Two-way intersection DID-INT is the simplest case. As the effects of the covariates are local to each $(s,t)$ group, separate regressions of the form:

\[Y_{i,s,t} = \lambda_{s,t} + \sum_{k=1}^{K} \gamma^k_{s,t} X^k_{i,s,t} + \epsilon_{i,s,t}\]

are estimated for each $(s,t)$ cell, where $\hat{\lambda}_{s,t}$ is simply the estimated intercept for the local regression.

If, for any $(s,t)$ group, a covariate causes the design matrix to be rank-deficient, then that covariate is dropped from the regression for that $(s,t)$ group, and its coefficient is taken to be zero in that group when computing the Integrated Long Differences. Covariates that do not vary within the group are dropped first, and any remaining collinear covariates are identified with a rank-revealing QR decomposition of the covariates centered at their group means. Centering matters because the regression has an intercept: covariates can be collinear with the intercept without being collinear with each other. The typical case is a categorical covariate whose reference category does not occur in the group, so that the group's dummy variables add up to one; one of them is then dropped in that group. The group contains no information about the absent category, and the estimates can then depend on which category was chosen as the reference.

Collinear Covariates

Dropping collinear covariates is a choice. The Python implementation of DID-INT (didintpy) instead keeps every covariate and uses the minimum-norm least squares solution. The two approaches agree when a covariate is constant within an $(s,t)$ group, but can otherwise yield different covariate coefficients, and therefore different ATT estimates, for groups with collinear covariates.

State-Varying, Time-Varying, and Homogeneous DID-INT

For state-varying, time-varying, and homogeneous DID-INT, the Frisch-Waugh-Lovell (FWL) theorem is applied. The procedure is the same across all three cases, differing only in the grouping over which the covariate coefficients $\hat{\beta}$ are estimated:

Covariates Measured at the State-Period Level

The covariate coefficients are estimated from the variation of the covariates within $(s,t)$ cells. A covariate that is constant within every cell, such as a variable measured at the state-period level, is perfectly collinear with the $I(s,t)$ cell dummies: it is absorbed by the $\hat{\lambda}_{s,t}$ values and has no effect on the estimates under any DID-INT variation.

  1. Both the outcome and covariates are demeaned within each $(s,t)$ cell - which is equivalent to residualizing the outcome and covariates by the $I(s,t)$ cell dummies.
  2. The covariate coefficients $\hat{\beta}$ are estimated by regressing the demeaned outcome on the demeaned covariates (without an intercept), where the regression is run:
    • Globally across all observations for homogeneous DID-INT
    • Within each time period $t$ for time-varying DID-INT
    • Within each state $s$ for state-varying DID-INT
  3. The $\hat{\lambda}_{s,t}$ values are then recovered as:

\[\hat{\lambda}_{s,t} = \overline{Y}_{s,t} - \hat{\beta}'\overline{X}_{s,t}\]

where $\overline{Y}_{s,t}$ and $\overline{X}_{s,t}$ are the cell means of the outcome and covariates for state $s$ in period $t$, respectively. This follows from taking expectations of the regression model conditional on $(s,t)$:

\[\mathbb{E}[Y_{i,s,t} \mid s,t] = \lambda_{s,t} + \beta'\mathbb{E}[X_{i,s,t} \mid s,t]\]

which gives $\overline{Y}_{s,t} = \hat{\lambda}_{s,t} + \hat{\beta}'\overline{X}_{s,t}$, and rearranging yields the recovery formula above.

Two One-Way DID-INT

Two one-way DID-INT follows the same FWL-based procedure as the preceding three cases, differing only in how step 2 is structured to reflect the additive nature of two one-way DID-INT:

  1. Both the outcome and covariates are demeaned within each $(s,t)$ cell - again, equivalent to residualizing by the $I(s,t)$ cell dummies.
  2. Because two one-way DID-INT implies that covariate effects are additively separable into a state-specific component, $\hat{\beta}_s$, and a time-specific component, $\hat{\beta}_t$, both sets of coefficients must be estimated jointly. This is done by regressing the demeaned outcome $\tilde{Y}_{i,s,t}$ (where $\tilde{Y}_{i,s,t} = Y_{i,s,t} - \overline{Y}_{s,t}$) on a structured sparse design matrix $Z$ of dimension $n \times (n_s + n_t)K$. The matrix $Z$ consists of $n_s + n_t$ blocks of $K$ columns each - one block per state, followed by one block per time period. For observation $i$ in cell $(s,t)$, the demeaned covariates $\tilde{X}_{i,s,t}$ are placed in the $s$-th state block and the $t$-th time block, with all other entries in that row set to zero. The regression model used to recover $\hat{\beta}_s$ and $\hat{\beta}_t$ is given by: $\tilde{Y}_{i,s,t} = \hat{\beta}'Z_{i,s,t} + \varepsilon_{i,s,t}$, where the first $n_{s} \cdot K$ entries of $\hat{\beta}$ correspond to the $\hat{\beta}_s$ coefficients, and the following $n_{t} \cdot K$ entries of $\hat{\beta}$ correspond to the $\hat{\beta}_t$ coefficients.
  3. The $\hat{\lambda}_{s,t}$ values are then recovered as:

\[\hat{\lambda}_{s,t} = \overline{Y}_{s,t} - \hat{\beta}_s'\overline{X}_{s,t} - \hat{\beta}_t'\overline{X}_{s,t}\]

Computation of Edge Case Standard Errors

In general, the heteroskedasticity-robust standard error of an $\widehat{\mathrm{ATT}}(s,t)$ is retrieved directly from the Step 3 regression. However, as noted in the Standard Errors and P-Values section, when the treated state has only one control state in that period the regression has two observations and two parameters, the model is saturated, and the regression-based standard error is degenerate. In such cases, the variance of the ATT can instead be recovered from the variances and covariances of the $\hat{\lambda}_{s,t}$ values, as shown in the decomposition in the aforementioned section. This is referred to as the edge case.

The edge case is a property of an individual $\widehat{\mathrm{ATT}}(s,t)$, whatever the aggregation method and whether adoption is common or staggered. The edge case standard errors therefore appear in the results:

  • under "sgt" aggregation, for every $\widehat{\mathrm{ATT}}(s,t)$ with a single control state;
  • for any subaggregate ATT that consists of a single $\widehat{\mathrm{ATT}}(s,t)$ with a single control state (and for the aggregate ATT if that is the only subaggregate ATT).

Subaggregate ATTs that average two or more $\widehat{\mathrm{ATT}}(s,t)$ values obtain their standard error from the spread of those values, as described in the Standard Errors and P-Values section, and do not involve the edge case. The jackknife and the randomization inference procedure only use point estimates and are never affected. An edge case standard error is reported without a p-value, as the saturated regression leaves no residual degrees of freedom for a $t$-distribution.

Recovering the variance of the ATT from the variances and covariances of the $\hat{\lambda}_{s,t}$ values necessitates computing the variance-covariance matrix of the $\hat{\lambda}_{s,t}$ values, $\widehat{\mathrm{Var}}(\hat{\lambda})$. This is a more computationally expensive procedure than the standard lambda estimation procedure, so by default DiDInt.jl does not compute it. Computation of edge case standard errors can be toggled on by setting edgecase = true. When edge case computation is toggled on, the set of required $\hat{\lambda}_{s,t}$ values needed to compute ATTs is determined prior to any computations, and those involved in the saturated Step 3 regression(s) are identified. Only the necessary blocks of data are sent to the edge case computation procedure, where both the $\hat{\lambda}_{s,t}$ values and their variances/covariances are recovered. The separation of the data into blocks sent to the edge case procedure, and the rest of the data which is fed into the standard Estimating Lambda procedure is determined by the $(s,t)$ groups to which the implicated $\hat{\lambda}_{s,t}$ belong to as well as the specific DID-INT variation. For example, time-varying DID-INT requires that all observations in group $t$ are grouped together, so any observation belonging to a $t$ period implicated in a saturated Step 3 regression is sent to the edge case procedure. Parallel logic applies to the other DID-INT variations, however it should be noted that the edge case procedure is not an option for two one-way DID-INT as this model is always rank deficient and $\widehat{\mathrm{Var}}(\hat{\lambda})$ is not recoverable.

Computing $\widehat{\mathrm{Var}}(\hat{\lambda})$

The variance-covariance matrix $\widehat{\mathrm{Var}}(\hat{\lambda})$ is an $n_\lambda \times n_\lambda$ matrix, where $n_\lambda = n_s \cdot n_t$ is the total number of $\hat{\lambda}_{s,t}$ values. For homogeneous DID-INT it is computed by following a single regression, and for all other DID-INT variations it is computed in blocks following regressions on subsets of the data. The raw outcome $Y_{i,s,t}$ is regressed on $I(s,t)$ cell dummies and the original (non-demeaned) covariates $X$, after which the covariance matrix of $\hat{\lambda}_{s,t}$ values from that regression is recovered using the chosen HCCME.

The specific regression used to compute $\widehat{\mathrm{Var}}(\hat{\lambda})$ mirrors the CCC structure of the chosen DID-INT variant, which also determines which of the terms of the variance decomposition in the Standard Errors and P-Values section can be nonzero:

  • Two-way intersection ("int"): a separate regression is run for each $(s,t)$ cell, with the cell dummy and covariates as regressors. The HCCME for each cell's dummy coefficient is placed into the corresponding diagonal entry of $\widehat{\mathrm{Var}}(\hat{\lambda})$. Off-diagonal entries between different cells are zero since the regressions are run separately, so only the four variance terms of the decomposition remain.
  • State-varying ("state"): a separate regression is run for each state $s$, pooling all time periods within that state. The HCCME for the cell dummies within each state block is placed into the corresponding entries of $\widehat{\mathrm{Var}}(\hat{\lambda})$ (including off-diagonal covariance terms). The covariances between the two periods of the same state are nonzero; those between the treated state and the control state are zero.
  • Time-varying ("time"): analogous to state-varying, with separate regressions run for each time period $t$. The covariances between the treated state and the control state in the same period are nonzero; all others are zero.
  • Homogeneous ("hom"): a single global regression is run across all observations. The HCCME for all cell dummies from the regression is exactly $\widehat{\mathrm{Var}}(\hat{\lambda})$, and every term of the decomposition can be nonzero.

If one of these regressions has no residual degrees of freedom or is not of full rank, the corresponding variances cannot be computed and the edge case standard error is returned as missing. It is also missing when the estimated variance of one of the four $\hat{\lambda}_{s,t}$ values is exactly zero (for example a binary outcome that takes a single value in one of the cells), and, under HC2, HC3, and HC4, when a block regression includes a cell with a single observation (that observation has a leverage of one). Under HC1, the degrees-of-freedom correction is the one of each block regression.

Edge Case Standard Errors for Two-Way Intersection DID-INT with Covariates

For homogeneous, state-varying, and time-varying DID-INT, the covariate term of the Integrated Long Differences cancels, $\widehat{\mathrm{ATT}}(s,t)$ is a combination of four $\hat{\lambda}_{s,t}$ values with fixed coefficients, and the decomposition above gives its variance. For two-way intersection DID-INT with covariates the covariate term does not cancel. Since each cell's regression passes through the cell means, $\hat{\lambda}_{s,t} + \hat{\gamma}_{s,t}'\overline{X}_{s,t} = \overline{Y}_{s,t}$, and with a single control state $c$:

\[\widehat{\mathrm{ATT}}(s,t) = \left(\overline{Y}_{s,t} - \hat{\delta}'\,\overline{X}_{s,t}\right) - \left(\hat{\lambda}_{s,r} + \hat{\lambda}_{c,t} - \hat{\lambda}_{c,r}\right), \qquad \hat{\delta} = \hat{\gamma}_{s,r} + \hat{\gamma}_{c,t} - \hat{\gamma}_{c,r}\]

The term $\hat{\delta}'\,\overline{X}_{s,t}$ is a product of two estimates: covariate coefficients estimated from three cells, and covariate means estimated from the treated state's cell in period $t$. DiDInt.jl treats $\overline{X}_{s,t}$ as an estimate (of the mean of the covariate distribution that the long differences are integrated over) rather than as fixed, and computes:

\[\mathrm{Var}(\widehat{\mathrm{ATT}}(s,t)) = \mathrm{Var}(\overline{Z}_{s,t}) + \sum_{k \in \{(s,r),\,(c,t),\,(c,r)\}} a' V_k\, a + \mathrm{tr}\!\left(V_{\delta}\, \Sigma\right)\]

where:

  • \[\overline{Z}_{s,t}\]

    is the mean of $Z_i = Y_i - \hat{\delta}' X_i$ over the observations of cell $(s,t)$, and $\mathrm{Var}(\overline{Z}_{s,t})$ is its HCCME variance. It accounts for the sampling variation in $\overline{Y}_{s,t}$ and in $\overline{X}_{s,t}$, and for their covariance.

  • \[a = (1, \overline{X}_{s,t})\]

    , and $V_k$ is the HCCME covariance matrix of the intercept and covariate coefficients from the regression of cell $k$.

  • \[V_{\delta}\]

    is the sum of the covariance matrices of the covariate coefficients of the three cells, and $\Sigma$ is the HCCME covariance matrix of $\overline{X}_{s,t}$. The trace term is the variance of the product of the estimation errors in $\hat{\delta}$ and $\overline{X}_{s,t}$; it is small unless cells have very few observations.

Independence Across Cells

Panel Data

Every edge case standard error assumes that observations in different $(s,t)$ cells are independent of each other. This holds for repeated cross-sections, where each period samples different individuals. It does not hold for panel data, where the same individuals are observed in several periods: the estimates for a state's periods are then correlated, and the formulas above omit that covariance (under two-way intersection DID-INT with covariates, this includes the covariance between $\overline{X}_{s,t}$ and the same state's estimates for the period before treatment). With panel data the edge case standard errors should be interpreted with caution.

Consistency with Lambda Estimation

For the FWL-based cases ("hom", "state", "time"), covariates that are constant within every $(s,t)$ cell in the relevant block are dropped from the block regressions used in the edge case procedure. These same covariates are demeaned to exactly zero during the regular lambda estimation procedure and thus contribute nothing to the estimated $\hat{\beta}$ there either. For the "int" case, both procedures are identical: any covariate that does not vary within a given $(s,t)$ cell is dropped from that cell's regression in both the normal lambda estimation procedure and the edge case procedure.

Rank Deficiency and Lambda Identification

In all DID-INT variations besides two-way intersection DID-INT, the design matrices used to recover $\hat{\lambda}_{s,t}$ values are allowed to be rank deficient, meaning $\hat{\lambda}_{s,t}$ is not always uniquely identified. However, the ATT estimates remain uniquely identified due to cancellation that occurs in the long difference contrasts. DiDInt.jl makes use of the \ operator while computing $\hat{\lambda}_{s,t}$ values in order to facilitate this, which returns the minimum-norm least squares solution, ensuring consistent ATT estimates between procedures, even when $\hat{\lambda}_{s,t}$ is not uniquely identified. In the case of two-way intersection DID-INT, in both the normal lambda estimation procedure and the edge case procedure, a rank revealing QR procedure is used to drop any remaining rank deficiency inducing covariates within each $(s,t)$ block after having already removed covariates that did not vary within the given $(s,t)$ block.

Plotting with didint_plot()

didint_plot() is intended as a simple visual tool. For each requested ccc option it plots the $\hat{\lambda}_{s,t}$ values from Step 1 of the Four Step Estimation Procedure (equation 45 of the DID-INT paper) by state and period, or, for event study plots, the averages of the treated states' $\hat{\lambda}_{s,t}$ values by periods since treatment. These show how each form of covariate adjustment affects the trends in the outcome. They are not the Integrated Long Differences used by didint(), so the plots are not meant to reproduce the ATT estimates exactly (the two differ when ccc = "int" is used with covariates).
